1. INTRODUCTION  
 
The World Health Organization (WHO) and Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) define a 
food commodity as safe when free from all hazards, which may make food injurious to the health 
of the consumers whether for chronic or acute consequences (FAO 2003). This concept may 
assume a number of conjugations that include both microbiological, chemical and physical hazards 
and nutritional aspects.  
 
The food trade has evolved rapidly in the recent past because of emerging economic policies, 
industrialization, globalization, urbanization, and online trade. The processed food market is 
growing, while more people are relying on commercially available ready-to-eat food. In the 
meantime, more chemical substances are used at the agricultural and animal husbandry level. 
Hence, the use of chemicals at the primary food production level and various stages of processing, 
storage and transport poses a challenge for food safety under this situation.  
 
Safe food is a primary determinant of human health and having access to safe, quality and healthy 
food is a basic human right. Assurance of food safety is a key driving force for better human health 
and for trading of safe and quality food. It is vital to highlight that food security cannot be achieved 
without food safety, and therefore food security and food safety are inextricably linked.  
 
1.1  Background  
 
Food Safety is a key component of the public health and falls under the mandate of the Ministry 
of Health (MoH). The food safety control system of the country currently operates based on the 
legislative framework stipulated under the Food Act enacted by the Parliament 
in 1980.  Accordingly, the main administrative responsibility for food safety lies with the Ministry 
of Health (MoH), with the Director General of Health Services serving as the Chief Food 
Authority (CFA). In addition, there are food authorities, identified by the Food Act. Food 
Authorities are persons responsible to ensure the implementing of the Food Act and its regulation 
to ensure food safety in his administrative area / sector as specified by the Food Act. The Food 
Control Administration Unit (FCAU) is the central body under the Ministry of Health that 
oversees and coordinates food safety in the country.  
 
Different food safety data reveal that there is a gap in controlling contaminants that are originating 
at the primary food production level, food processing and storage level or unsatisfactory cold 
chain maintenance during the food chain.

1.2 Purpose & Context 
 
The current country context in relation to food is defined by the expansion of the global food 
trade, new concepts, agreements, and interactions as well as lifestyles and consumption habits. 
This has changed the food safety focus and approaches to accept international food standards 
and norms.  
 
Foodborne diseases still remain as a public health problem. Although, food regulations to 
maintain Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) and Good Hygienic Practices (GHP) are in place, 
implementation of such regulations such as Food (hygiene) and Food (premises registration) 
regulations are relatively poor compared to the implementation of other food regulations. Hence, 
priority should be given to implementing GMP and GHP according to the regulations throughout 
the food chain and Hazard Analyses and Critical Control Point (HACCP) should be strengthened 
to prevent food borne illnesses.  
 
The lack of knowledge and financial constraints to invest better food safety mechanisms are 
potential reasons specially in the Small and Medium Enterprises (SME) sector as well as in street 
food sector. Lack of formal food handlers’ registration system following a basic food safety 
training have also been identified as a gap in the system. Inadequate laboratory facilities 
especially in the government sector, seriously impact ensuring domestic food safety control. Lack 
of a reference food laboratory and inadequate laboratory facilities to implement newly developed 
food regulations have been identified as major issues related to food testing. In addition, lack of 
human capacity at the government laboratories have led to utilization of private food laboratories 
which has created potential food safety issues, especially at the border control. 
 
All players of the food chain need varying levels of information and capacity to ensure food 
safety. It could vary from basic food safety knowledge to scientific risk analyses, management, 
and risk communication as well as practical knowledge on monitoring and implementation of risk 
management system such as HACCP. Therefore, it is emphasized that capacity building of the 
players of the food chain is a priority and a responsibility of all relevant sectors, including food 
industries.  
 
Food transport and storage is a segment of the food chain which has been identified as poorly 
regulated and not directly under any key government agency. Therefore, special focus should be 
made to prevent food safety during food storage and transport, especially for perishable food and 
food with high risk of fungal growth resulting in toxin contamination. Many informal food outlets, 
cooked food transportation and food redistribution mechanisms have commenced following the 
COVID-19 pandemic and economic crisis. Accordingly, new food safety issues and challenges 
have begun during the recent past. Therefore, assurance of food safety in these new food 
innovations including street food sector require specific food safety measures.

As food safety information are very sensitive, only the proven and estimated risks must be 
communicated to the community to avoid unwarranted fear which may cause undue public unrest 
and food insecurity. Formal risk analysis is the scientific method behind risk assessment, risk 
management and risk communication which need a specially trained team and resources. 
However, the lack of capability on risk analysis of food safety hazards is a major drawback in the 
current context. The lack of efficient systems to address social unrest and public complaints also 
have been identified as a key issue in the current food safety system.  
 
Unethical food marketing practices and the promotion of food that are not identified as a 
component of a healthy diet, specially to pregnant and lactating mothers and children have been 
identified as another problem, which has been proven as one determinant for child obesity and 
long-term health consequences. Global evidence shows that availability of robust regulations and 
strict enforcement restricts consumption of unhealthy food.  Therefore, it is emphasized and 
expected food industries to understand their national responsibility related to food marketing, 
comply with food regulations and maintain food ethics in fair trade.  
 
1.3 Rationale  
 
Despite a widened scope of the food sector and its operators, at present, there is no available 
policy on food safety to guide and streamline the food safety system at the national level. Ensuring 
the safety of food is essential for maintaining an acceptable standard of healthy living. Hence, it 
is government's responsibility to establish a sustainable system that ensures the safety and quality 
of food available for both national and international consumers. Hence, a robust food 
safety control mechanism should be introduced incorporating new approaches to the existing 
system and in aligned with international food safety management systems to increase 
consumer acceptance locally and globally. 
 
Some major issues identified in the existing food control system include inadequate responsibility 
and accountability for food safety by key stakeholders, deficient intersectoral monitoring and 
coordination mechanism, lack of adequate laboratory facilities and inadequate implementation of 
food safety activities due to lack of officers exclusively dedicated for food safety at the national 
and peripheral levels.  
 
Food safety cannot be achieved only through end-product testing. Hence, preventative measures 
must be implemented throughout the food chain as many food safety hazards are originating 
somewhere at the food chain from farm to table. It is important to note that some of the 
contaminants such as aflatoxins, heavy metals and pesticide residues originating at the primary 
food production level, and cannot be rectified at a later stage. Therefore, ensuring food safety at 
the primary food production level are essential with special emphasis on promoting Good

Agricultural Practices (GAP) and Good Animal Husbandry Practices (GAHP), through 
establishing district food authorities at the primary food production level and empowering them 
under the Food Act. Strengthening preventative food safety approaches at all levels would ensure 
not only the food safety, but food security and facilitation of safe food export. The Situational 
Analysis Report (2023) on food safety control system in Sri Lanka was prepared under the overall 
supervision of the Ministry of Health in collaboration with Food and Agriculture Organization and funded 
by the European Union. The report highlighted several structural and operational deficiencies, including 
inadequate staffing, lack of specialized technical expertise, and fragmentation across multiple technical 
units. The report recommended establishing a Food Safety Bureau under the Chief Food Authority 
(DGHS). As such the Food Act is to be updated providing provisions to establish a high level 
multisectoral committee (Food Safety Council) with sharing responsibility and accountability of 
ensuring food safety of the country. The legal power under the Food Act for supervising and 
monitoring of the district level food authorities to be given to the relevant department heads who 
would represent the Food Safety Council. Monitoring and coordination committees also need to 
be included to the existing food safety framework for effective monitoring, coordination and 
implementation of the food safety policy and strategies. Well-established food safety systems 
contribute to improved public health, increased food security, protection of environment, 
increased demand for food trade and promote sustainable economic development.  
 
2. POLICY PRINCIPLES  
1. Access to safe, quality, and healthy food through governance and surveillance. 
2. Risk-based, evidence- driven, preventative food safety approaches. 
3. Public health approach and multi-sectoral collaboration for food safety. 
4. Community engagement and empowerment. 
5. Transparency, traceability, and equity of food safety system. 
3. POLICY STATEMENTS  
 
1. A national food safety governance framework in place to ensure safe and quality food to 
promote and protect public health and facilitate domestic and international food trade. 
2. Food safety legislation and standards across the food chain are aligned with scientific evidence 
and national requirements.  
3. National food safety surveillance and, risk analysis, are in operation to respond to food safety 
emergencies and challenges. 
4. Sustainable mechanisms established to implement food safety policy and to address food 
safety challenges effectively.

4. POLICY GOALS 
 
Ensure safe, healthy, and quality food to protect health of national and international consumers 
and to develop economic growth through a coordinated, collaborative, and accountable approach 
among stakeholders throughout the food chain.  
 
5. APPLICABILITY & SCOPE  
 
The Ministry of Health (MoH) is mandated with ensuring food safety in the country. Hence, the 
policy recognizes Director General of Health Service as the Chief Food Authority (CFA) and 
Chair of the proposed Food Safety Council. MoH collaborates and coordinates with other relevant 
agencies including private sector and consumers to promote and protect public health and to 
facilitate fair food trade.  
 
Food safety has a broader concept and encompasses prevention of consumers from all kinds of 
health hazards in food. The food safety policy covers the entire food chain, including primary 
production sectors incorporating small, medium, and large enterprises, transportation, storage, 
processing, preparation, advertising, sale, and distribution for consumption to ensure safe, quality, 
and healthy food through promulgation and enforcement of appropriate laws and regulations, 
aligned with scientific evidence and country requirements. The policy integrates all pertinent 
sectors and stakeholders, both from the government and private entities emphasizing shared 
responsibility and accountability. The policy also engages and empowers the community with 
food safety information, education, and communication.  
 
Furthermore, the policy guides the provincial, district and divisional bodies to ensure effective 
policy implementation through monitoring and coordination at central and peripheral level. This 
policy is coherent with the upcoming National Policy on Health and Wellbeing 2026-2035 and 
other health policies such as National Nutrition Policy 2021-2030 and National Policy on 
Maternal Child Health. It also complements other policies related to food safety and global needs 
leading to an improved food safety management system.

6. POLICY IMPLEMENTATION  
 
The National Food Safety Policy is planned to be implemented over a period of ten years from 
2025 to 2035. The Ministry of Health and Mass Media is the leading authority to oversee the 
implementation of the policy in close collaboration with other responsible agencies and 
consumers.

6.2 Responsibility and authority  
 
National Level 
• The administrative authority for the food safety policy rests with the Director General of 
Health Services (DGHS), who serves as the Chief Food Authority (CFA).   
• The current Food Control and Administrative Unit of the Ministry of Health to be expanded 
as the Food Safety Bureau (FSB) under the Ministry of Health. 
 
Provincial Level 
• Provincial level food safety monitoring and coordination committee, headed by the Provincial 
Directors of Health Services (PDHS) and composed of heads of the relevant sectors to be 
established with the responsibility of monitoring and coordinating policy implementation and 
preventing of food safety hazards arising from their administrative areas.  
 
District Level.  
• It is proposed that RDHS also to be appointed as Food Authority.  
• Food authorities and authorized officers for selected primary food production sectors to be 
established at district level with the responsibility of preventing food safety hazards arising 
from their sectors.  They will be monitored and supervised by the head of the department who 
will be a member of the FSC 
 
Divisional / Ground Level 
• Medical Officer of Health (MOH) and the Municipal Councils will be the Food Authorities at 
divisional level.  
• Existing authorized officers under the Food Act will remain without any change. 
• A mechanism to be developed to ensure that all authorized officers working under Municipal 
Councils to be legally responsible to the relevant MOH with regards to food safety of 
municipal council areas (to be further discussed).

7. GLOSSARY  
Animal Feed: Food whether processed, semi-processed or raw, given to domestic animals, 
especially livestock, in the course of animal husbandry  
Authorized Officer: Officer appointed under a Food Authority to implement the Food Act and its 
regulations by enforcing legal powers including inspection and prosecution. 
Codex Alimentarius: Collection of standards, guidelines and codes of practice adopted by the Codex 
Alimentarius Commission. 
Chief Food Authority: Person responsible to ensure the implementing of the Food Act and its 
regulation to ensure food safety in the entire country. 
Contaminant: Any biological, or chemical agent, foreign matter, other substances, not 
intentionally added to food which may compromise food safety or suitability  
Food Authority: Person responsible to ensure the implementing of the Food Act and its regulation to 
ensure food safety in in his administrative area. 
Food: Any substance, whether processed, semi-processed or raw, which is intended for human 
consumption, and includes drink, chewing gum and any substance which has been used in the 
manufacture, preparation or treatment of "food" but does not include cosmetics or tobacco or substances 
used only as drugs. 
Food Chain: The series of processes that involves set of people, Institutions, industries, infrastructure 
where food is grown or produced, sold, and eventually consumed. 
Food control system: Collection of activities carried out by the government to ensure food safety 
throughout the food chain.  
Food handler: any person who directly handles packaged or unpackaged food, food equipment and 
utensils or food contact surfaces and is therefore expected to comply with food hygiene requirements 
Food hygiene: all conditions, measures, and practices necessary to ensure the safety and suitability of 
food at all stages of the food chain  
Food premises: includes any shop, stall, or place where a food is sold or manufactured or stored for 
sale

Food safety: Food safety is defined as “the assurance that food will not cause harm to the consumer 
when it is prepared and/or eaten according to its intended use 
Food surveillance: Continuous, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of food safety 
information and timely sharing of such information with relevant stakeholders with the objective of 
risk assessment and taking control / preventive measures.  
Food borne disease: Illnesses contracted from eating contaminated food or beverages. Illnesses 
include food borne intoxication and infections ranging from diarrhoea to cancers 
Hazard: A biological, chemical, or physical agent in or condition of food with the potential to cause 
and adverse health effect  
HACCP: A system which provides the framework for monitoring the total food system, from 
harvesting to consumption, to reduce the risk of food borne illness. The system is designed to identify 
and control potential problems before they occur. 
Risk assessment: The scientific evaluation of known or potential adverse health effects resulting from 
human exposure to food borne hazards. 
Risk assessment team: Scientific multidisciplinary team appointed by the CFA with the objective of 
food safety risk assessment and advise on risk management and communication.  
Risk communication:  refers to the interactive exchange of information and opinions throughout the 
risk analysis process concerning hazards and risks, risk-related factors and risk perceptions, among risk 
assessors, risk managers, consumers, industry, the academic community and other interested parties, 
including the explanation of risk assessment findings and the basis of risk management decisions. 
Risk management: Involves both the identification of the standards of acceptable risk appropriate to 
different types of food hazards, and the establishment of procedures to ensure that the risks are kept 
within the limits set by those standards
